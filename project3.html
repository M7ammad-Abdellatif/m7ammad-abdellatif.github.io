Overview

This project combines primary survey data from students and academic supervisors with a 197K-record university dataset to better understand what drives academic performance. Unlike traditional approaches that rely only on historical data, it incorporates behavioral and socioeconomic context to capture factors the data alone cannot explain. The system analyzes these combined signals to identify key performance drivers and support early identification of at-risk students.

Data Sources

Self-Collected Survey + OULAD

Two sources were collected and used in parallel — a self-collected survey and an external academic dataset. Each answers different questions about student behavior.

Primary
Google Forms Survey
Distributed to students and academic supervisors. Captured perceptions around submission timing, attempt frequency, psychological factors, and how SES affects study habits. Responses collected from both student and supervisor perspectives, then analyzed qualitatively via Google Forms charts.
Secondary
Open University Learning Analytics
Kaggle dataset sourced from the Open University (UK). 197,778 student records merged from two sub-tables: studentInfo and studentAssessment. Covers demographics, submission dates, IMD deprivation bands, assessment scores, and course attempt history.

Pipeline

Collect → Clean → Correlate → Model

The pipeline runs end-to-end in Python, guided by a mixed-method research approach that combines qualitative survey insights with large-scale academic data to better capture real-world factors influencing performance.

01
Survey Design & Distribution
Google Forms survey built with two role-specific sections — one for students, one for supervisors. Distributed via email and direct outreach at HTU. Responses collected from both groups; supervisor responses required in-person follow-up to reach adequate volume.
02
Dataset Acquisition & Merging
Open University dataset downloaded from Kaggle. Two relevant sub-tables (studentInfo, studentAssessment) merged into a single working dataset. Irrelevant tables from the 7-table schema discarded.
03
Cleaning & Encoding
Nulls handled, categorical features encoded (one-hot for region, ordinal for IMD and education level). Resulting dataset: 197,778 rows × 23 columns. Both clean and unclean versions saved.
04
Correlation Analysis
Pearson correlations computed between each feature and the target variable (score). Three focal dimensions analyzed: submission timing, socioeconomic indicators (IMD band, education level, region), and number of previous attempts.
05
Model Training & Tuning
Four model families trained: Linear Regression (with RFE iteration), KNN (with feature scaling, Grid Search, Randomized Search), Random Forest (baseline + iteration + Randomized Search), Gradient Boosting (baseline + Grid Search + 50-iteration tuning). Each evaluated on MAE, MSE, RMSE, and R².
06
Qualitative Analysis
Survey response charts from Google Forms reviewed and interpreted alongside the quantitative findings. Used to triangulate statistical patterns against first-hand perceptions from students and supervisors.
Correlation Analysis

What drives student performance (and what doesn't)

Pearson correlations were computed between the target variable (score) and all features. No single factor strongly explains performance — outcomes are multi-causal.

highest_education
+0.084
Strongest single predictor. Students with higher educational backgrounds tend to score slightly better. Positive but weak.
imd_band
+0.080
Students from less deprived areas (higher IMD band) tend toward marginally higher scores. SES has a real but modest effect.
num_of_prev_attempts
-0.077
More previous attempts weakly correlates with lower scores — possibly signaling that repeated failure reflects underlying comprehension gaps.
date_submitted
-0.036
Later submissions correlate with slightly lower scores. The effect is real but small — submission timing alone doesn't determine outcomes.
region (various)
≈ 0.00
Regional features showed negligible correlation (–0.023 to +0.012). Geography matters far less than SES or education level.
Overall signal
Weak
Performance isn't driven by one variable — it emerges from a combination of behavior, background, and context. This explains why simple models struggle.
Modeling

Can student performance actually be predicted?

Four model families were tested (Linear Regression, KNN, Random Forest, Gradient Boosting), but overall predictive performance remained limited.

①
Linear models performed poorly
Linear Regression failed to capture meaningful patterns, indicating the relationships in the data are not linear.
②
Tree-based models performed better
Tree-based models (Random Forest, Gradient Boosting) performed better by capturing non-linear patterns, but improvements were not strong enough for reliable predictions.
③
Overall predictive signal was still limited
The best model explained only ~25% of performance variance, suggesting key factors are missing. Important drivers like motivation, engagement, and study behavior are not captured in the dataset.
Student performance is not easily predictable using static features alone — it depends on dynamic behavior and context that typical datasets don’t capture.
Key Insights
→ Earlier submissions slightly improve performance, effect is real but weak (−0.036), supported by both data and survey responses.
→ Socioeconomic factors have a modest impact IMD and education level correlate positively, but effects remain small.
→ Repeated attempts signal lower performance (–0.077), likely reflecting underlying comprehension gaps rather than effort.
→ Tree-based ensembles (RF, GBT) significantly outperform linear models on this dataset — suggesting the relationships between student features and outcomes are non-linear and interactive.
→ Survey and data align qualitative responses reinforce patterns observed in the dataset.
What I took from this
→ Running both a survey and model analysis on the same problem forced me to deal with conflicting signals. When both pointed in the same direction (like submission timing and SES), it felt reliable. When they didn’t, it made me question which signal actually mattered.
→ Collecting primary data was harder than expected. Getting academic supervisors to respond required multiple in-person follow-ups, which made it clear that designing a study is very different from actually executing it.
→ The low R² across models wasn’t surprising after seeing the correlations. No single feature explained much. The issue wasn’t the models — it was that the dataset doesn’t capture the factors that actually drive performance.
→ Hyperparameter tuning had a noticeable impact, especially for KNN and Gradient Boosting. Randomized Search consistently gave better results than Grid Search with less time, which is something I’d apply again in similar situations.
