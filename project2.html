Overview

Logistics delays are difficult to diagnose — shipments can leave on time, pass through hubs, and still arrive late without a clear cause. This system analyzes over 100,000 shipment records using PySpark to uncover what actually drives delays across a logistics network.

The pipeline processes raw data at scale, applies transformations and aggregations, and surfaces patterns across routes, transport modes, congestion levels, and departure timing. The analysis focuses on practical questions: which routes are consistently slower, how departure timing impacts delivery performance, and how congestion at origin hubs contributes to delays.

The system focuses on identifying:

  • Routes that consistently show higher delays
  • The impact of departure timing on delays
  • How congestion at origin hubs affects delivery performance
Dataset

Gulf Region Shipment Records

The dataset contains shipment records across four major logistics hubs in the Gulf region — Dubai (DXB01), Riyadh (RUH05), Jeddah (JED20), and Bahrain (BAH12) — covering operations throughout 2024. Each record captures the full journey: planned vs. actual departure and arrival, route, transport mode, cargo characteristics, and live congestion indices for both origin and destination hubs.

Pipeline

Ingest → Clean → Aggregate → Visualize

Everything runs through a single PySpark session, keeping compute distributed from ingestion through aggregation. Pandas only enters the picture at visualization time, after Spark has already done the heavy lifting.

01
Ingestion & Schema Inspection
Raw CSV loaded into PySpark with automatic schema inference. Schema printed and validated — 29 columns, 100,000 rows confirmed before any transformation begins.
02
Null Treatment & Deduplication
Per-column null sweep using a cast-to-int aggregation pattern. 446 rows dropped on critical fields (delay_minutes, actual_departure, actual_arrival, product_weight_kg, fragile). Zero duplicates found across all 100K records.
03
Feature Engineering & Column Pruning
11 redundant geo and label columns dropped. Scheduled departure parsed from string to timestamp, then departure hour extracted as a derived numeric feature using F.hour().
04
Aggregation & Pattern Analysis
GroupBy aggregations across four dimensions: origin congestion index, transport mode, departure hour, and route. Each slice computed avg delay and shipment count. Severely delayed shipments (>60 min) analyzed separately per mode.
05
Visualization
Aggregated DataFrames pulled into Pandas via .toPandas(). Line plots for trend analysis, bar charts for categorical comparisons — all built with Matplotlib and Seaborn.
Analysis & Visualizations

What the data showed

Four analyses were run across the cleaned dataset, each targeting a different dimension of delay. All aggregations ran on PySpark before being passed to Matplotlib/Seaborn for plotting.

Average Delay vs. Origin Congestion Index Trend
A clear upward trend emerges as congestion rises — from ~20–35 min at low levels (0.0–0.2) to ~50–55 min at the high end (0.8–1.0). The mid-range shows noise, suggesting congestion interacts with route-level factors rather than acting alone.
Avg Delay by Transport Mode Comparison
Sea freight shows the highest average delay (~43.8 min), followed by Van (40.2), Truck (38.0), and Air (34.1). The ~10-minute spread highlights a clear performance gap across transport modes.
Shipment Volume by Mode Volume
Truck dominates at 88K of 99.5K shipments. Van, Air, and Sea are minor channels — making truck operations the main driver of network-wide delay behavior.
Average Delay by Departure Hour Temporal
Two peaks emerge: morning (8–9am, ~43 min) and mid-afternoon (15–16h, ~41 min). Late-night and early-morning departures (0–5am) show the lowest delays (~29–32 min). This suggests peak-hour congestion at hubs drives delays — making departure timing one of the most actionable levers.
Average Delay by Route Route Analysis
DXB01–RUH05 is the worst-performing route (~43.4 min), with DXB01–BAH12 and JED20–DXB01 close behind (~42–41.5 min). RUH05–JED20 is a clear outlier in the opposite direction (~24.8 min), showing nearly half the delay — a structural difference, not random variation.
Predictive Modeling
Three regression models were trained to predict shipment delay: Linear Regression, Random Forest, and Gradient Boosted Trees. The full ML pipeline included StringIndexer, OneHotEncoder, VectorAssembler, and StandardScaler stages chained into a Pipeline object. Gradient Boosted Trees performed best, capturing non-linear relationships across route, timing, and congestion features.
  • StringIndexer — converts categorical labels into indexed numerical form
  • OneHotEncoder — expands categorical variables into sparse binary vectors
  • VectorAssembler — combines all features into a single feature vector
  • StandardScaler — normalizes feature scales for stable model training
What I took from this
→ Working with PySpark at 100K rows forced a different mental model than pandas. Instead of inspecting rows, you design transformations and reason about outputs before they materialize. That shift takes time to internalize.
→ The departure hour finding was the most surprising. A 10+ minute difference in average delay between 8am and 2am departures, consistent across the full year, is the kind of pattern that gets lost in aggregate statistics but is immediately actionable for a scheduling team.
→ The RUH05–JED20 route's performance gap (24.8 vs. 43+ min on every other corridor) raises a real question — whether it's distance, hub characteristics, or something else driving it. The data shows the pattern clearly; explaining the mechanism would need additional operational context.
→ Building the MLlib pipeline — chaining indexers, encoders, assemblers, and scalers into a single Pipeline — showed how Spark structures ML workflows differently from scikit-learn. More verbose, but built to scale.
